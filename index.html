STEM Racing (formerly F1 in Schools) asks student teams to design, build, and race a CO₂-powered car inside a tight rulebook. Our team formed two weeks before Regionals, qualified, and finished 4th at Nationals, about 10 points out of 1,000 short of the international final. I led the car's design and manufacturing and ran the team's operations and sponsorship.

Step 1 · Simulation method

Two solvers, used for different jobs

We had almost no track time, so the car was developed in CFD. I simplified each new geometry in Ansys Discovery and used its real-time solver to screen ideas in minutes. Only the designs that survived went to Ansys Fluent for a steady-state RANS run.

Freestream25 m/s, with the floor moving at 25 m/s
CO₂ jet80 m/s velocity inlet
WheelsRotating at 1,785 rad/s
DomainHalf car with a symmetry plane
Reynolds no.About 374,000 (L = 0.22 m)
Why this wayFluent is accurate but slow. Screening in Discovery first meant the expensive runs were spent only on designs worth measuring. The half-car domain gave the same answer for a fraction of the mesh.
Step 2 · Baseline study

Finding where the energy went

Instead of drawing several unrelated concepts, we built one baseline car and studied it closely. With guidance from our CFD mentors at KM Aero on reading the results, the baseline showed four problems:

  • The front wing made strong suction but lost energy at the sharp junctions between wing, endplate, and support.
  • Wake from the front wheels was being drawn under the floor.
  • Flow separated off the sidepods and re-entered the floor through openings ahead of the rear wheels.
  • The diffuser's suction peak arrived late and weak, which added pressure drag.
Why this wayOne controlled baseline let us change a single feature at a time and know which change caused which result.
Step 3 · Design iterations

Four front wings, three rear wings, three bodies

  • Front wing. The first wing used a high-downforce profile for stability. Once the sidepods were doing that job, I moved to a neutral profile and reshaped the endplates to push air outward around the front tires, keeping wheel wake out of the floor.
  • Sidepods. A blunt first version gave way to aggressive side scoops. CFD showed their sharp edges caused separation, and they were hard to machine consistently, so the final version is a smooth taper that keeps flow attached and shields the rear wheels.
  • Body. Wide and flat at first, then a trimmed nose to cut frontal area, then fully rounded edges for a teardrop shape with a small wake.
  • Rear wing. Started simple, gained a proper support structure, and was then refined to balance downforce against drag.

Under identical simulation conditions, drag fell from 0.45 N on the first design to 0.229 N on the final car.

Why this wayDrag grows with the square of speed, so frontal area and attached flow mattered more than adding downforce. Every change also had to stay machinable in foam.
Step 4 · Wheel system

Removing the axle

The first car used a rod through the body with a wheel pressed on each end. It was quick to build, but the body needed clearance channels for the rod, which added frontal area, and lining it up at the track took time.

The final design has no through-axle. Each SLS-printed wheel carries a toleranced bearing pocket, and matching recesses in the wheel and the chassis mount hold the bearing in place radially and axially. The wheel itself has a symmetric airfoil cross-section to reduce the drag of a spinning wheel.

Why this wayNo axle bore means no drag from it and a slimmer body. Setup at the track became a matter of seating the wheels instead of feeding and aligning a rod.
Step 5 · Manufacturing

Why we dropped 5-axis machining

The body's sidepod cutouts and blended curves pointed to 5-axis machining, and the CAM simulation agreed. The foam did not. Tooling foam is too weak to clamp without distorting, and any fixture blocked toolpaths, so the 5-axis setup created more variation than it removed.

The rules require every body to be cut from its own official foam block, bought from the STEM Racing supplier, so a ruined block costs both money and days. We moved to a 3-axis router with a vacuum table, which holds each block evenly across its whole base, and machined every body from four sides:

  • Roughing with trochoidal toolpaths to keep tool load constant.
  • Rest machining with smaller ball-nose cutters to clear what the roughing tool could not reach.
  • Finishing passes with a tight stepover, leaving ridges under 0.025 mm on the aerodynamic surfaces.

For printed parts I tested PLA and PETG for form studies, SLA resin for low-friction surfaces, and carbon-fibre-filled filament for high-stress parts.

Why this wayWith a limited supply of regulation blocks, a reliable process mattered more than fewer setups. When every car is the same shape, a faster run means the design improved, not the machining.
Step 6 · Operations and result

Running the team

We were invited two weeks before Regionals, so every other team had a head start. I ran the project so that six members and three helpers could work in parallel without waiting on each other.

  • Charter and scope. A project charter set the deliverables up front: race cars, two 10-page portfolios, a 10-minute presentation, and a zero-waste pit display. We wrote a second charter after qualifying, when the targets changed. Anything outside that list was cut.
  • Work breakdown. The work was split into four streams (car development, enterprise, branding, and competition) and then into tasks with an owner and acceptance criteria, such as rule compliance for the car and clarity for the portfolios.
  • Timeline. A shared Gantt chart kept the schedule visible to everyone. Regionals and Test Day were the checkpoints where we compared progress to plan and re-ordered tasks.
  • Risk register. Nine risks were rated by likelihood and impact, and the highest were handled first. Manufacturing delays and damage to the final car both rated 8, so we started machining early, kept buffer time, and built duplicate cars and spare parts.
  • Meetings. Weekly meetings around a physical board, where each setback was worked through until there was a way around it.
  • Budget. Money went first to manufacturing and testing, the areas that decide performance, with a contingency held back.

The risk planning was tested once. A rules mix-up during machining ruined a block and stopped production with no spare in stock. Our sponsor GH Precision drove out to source a replacement the same day and we were cutting again within hours.

Sponsorship. To fund a season of about $11,900, I built an outreach system across a list of more than 1,000 companies: cold calls, emailed proposals, and follow-ups. Every sponsor who signed on went into a stakeholder register recording the last contact, who on the team owned the relationship, and how that sponsor preferred to be reached, and we checked in with each one at milestones. It brought in $4,000 in cash and $7,600 of in-kind CNC machining and 3D printing from more than 15 sponsors, plus software and CFD mentorship.

Result. Four cars across the season: one for Regionals, a major redesign, and two matched final cars. At the 2026 National Championships we placed 4th overall, 1st in Pit Display, 2nd in Project Management, and 3rd in Brand Identity. The lighthouse at the back of the display is a model I designed and 3D printed.

Why this wayOn a short timeline the plan has to be visible. A register and a chart that everyone can see let people pick up the next task without asking, and the top-rated risks got attention while there was still time to act.

Part A · The builds

Twenty-two keyboards, each built to a brief

I run a small business building custom mechanical keyboards for clients. Each build starts with what the client wants: layout, typing feel, sound, look, and budget. I turn that into a parts specification, then source, modify, and assemble it.

  • Design. I design CNC-machined cases in Onshape and write the fabrication specs, then have them CNC-machined through PCBWay.
  • Build. I modify the switches, solder and assemble each board, and test every key.
  • Fix. When a board comes back with a dead key or a bad connection, I trace the fault and repair it.
Why this wayTwo people can want opposite things from the same layout. Writing the brief down first means every part choice can be checked against what that client asked for.
Part B · Case design

An O-ring mounted case, machined in aluminum

I designed this case in Onshape, and it was CNC-machined in aluminum through PCBWay for a client build. The model shows the aluminum body in red and a brass weight in gold. The client commissioned the case without the weight, so the brass part was designed but not made.

  • O-ring mount. A rubber O-ring is stretched around the edge of the plate and PCB assembly, and the assembly is pressed into the case. The O-ring is the only thing touching the case walls, so no screws pass through the plate.
  • Daughterboard pocket. The USB-C port sits on a small separate board that screws into its own pocket in the case and connects to the main PCB by a short cable, which runs in a milled channel.
  • Brass weight. The design has a brass plate fastened into a pocket in the base with two screws. It is an option the client can leave out, as this one did.
Why an O-ringRubber between the assembly and the case absorbs vibration, so every key feels and sounds the same instead of being stiffer near screw points. It also lets the assembly lift out by hand for repairs.
Why a daughterboardThe assembly floats on the O-ring, so a port soldered to the main PCB would move against the case opening and take the strain of every cable pull. Fixing the port to the case keeps it aligned, and a damaged port can be replaced without replacing the PCB.
Why brassBrass is about three times denser than aluminum. The added mass keeps the board planted on the desk and deepens its sound. It also adds cost, which is why it is optional.

Machined pen and pen box

Scope
Designed, drawn, and machined by me
Material
Aluminum and brass
Processes
Manual lathe, manual mill, CNC mill (Mastercam)
Tolerance
±0.005 in (0.13 mm)
Part A · The pen

Three turned parts and a standard refill

The pen is an aluminum barrel with a brass tip and a brass end cap, all turned on the manual lathe. The barrel is drilled through and tapped at both ends. The tip and cap have matching external threads cut on their shanks.

  • Threaded ends. Both brass parts unscrew, so the refill can be replaced and the pen comes fully apart.
  • Grip grooves. Grooves turned into the tip give the fingers something to hold on a polished part.
  • Chamfered mouths. The barrel openings are chamfered so the brass threads start cleanly instead of cross-threading.
  • Taper and dome. The tip tapers to the refill point and the cap is rounded, so nothing sharp sits at either end.
  • Relief grooves. The stepped necks near each end of the barrel mark the transition to the brass and break up the plain cylinder.
What made it hardThe threads on three separate parts have to line up on one axis. If the tapped holes are off-centre, the tip and cap sit crooked and the joint shows.
Part B · The box

A milled case with a built-in hinge

The base and lid are each milled from solid aluminum. The base has a two-level pocket: a wide recess, and inside it a narrower slot sized to the pen so it cannot roll. The slot ends and pocket corners are rounded to the cutter radius, which also leaves no sharp inside corner to collect dirt.

  • Hinge. Two posts are left standing on the back corners of the base. A machine screw through each post acts as a hinge pin, so the box needs no separate hinge hardware.
  • Rounded posts. The tops of the posts are filleted. A square corner would catch the lid as it swings; the radius gives it room to rotate freely.
  • Lid. The lid is notched at both ends to clear the posts, so it closes flush with the base instead of resting on top of them.
  • Engraving. The lid artwork and my name were programmed in Mastercam and cut on the CNC mill.
  • Edges. Outside edges are chamfered so the box is comfortable to handle.
What made it hardThe hinge only works if the two screw holes share one axis and the lid notches match the posts. That fit is where the ±0.005 in tolerance mattered most.
Review

What went wrong, and what I would change

Both parts work, but three things did not come out the way I drew them.

DefectWhat happenedNext time
Refill protrudes too farI drilled the refill bore in the tip past its target depth. The refill seats further forward than designed, so the writing point sticks out more than it should.Set the depth with the quill stop, drill short, and test-fit the refill before taking the bore to final depth.
Hinge screws bindThe tapped holes in the box came out tight. The tap drill and tap did not run true to the hole axis, so the threads are undersized and slightly off-axis, and the screws are stiff to drive.Spot drill first, confirm the tap drill size, and guide the tap from the spindle so it starts square.
Rough pocket floor and wallsHeat built up at the cutter while milling the pocket, and the surface finish suffered. That added a lot of hand sanding and polishing.Use cutting fluid, clear chips more often, take lighter passes, and leave a shallow finishing pass at the end.
Sheet 04

Tic-tac-toe board

Scope
Designed, drawn, and machined by me
Material
Aluminum board, aluminum and brass pegs
Processes
Milling, drilling, countersinking, turning
Parts
1 board, 10 pegs

Two metals so the players can be told apart

This was my first project that needed parts to fit each other. The board is one aluminum block with a raised playing field and a lower tray at each end.

  • Pegs. Five brass and five aluminum pegs replace X and O. Each is turned with a shoulder and a locating pin, so it drops into a hole and stands upright.
  • Holes. All nineteen holes are countersunk. The cone guides a peg in even when it is dropped off-centre, and removes the sharp edge.
  • Clearance. The pins are a loose sliding fit in the holes, so a peg lifts out with two fingers and never jams.
  • Trays. Each end tray stores one player's five pegs, so the set stays together.
  • Grid. The lines are milled grooves, so they will not wear off.
What I learnedTen pegs have to fit nineteen holes in any combination. That is only possible if every pin diameter and every hole is held to the same size, which is what a tolerance is for.
Review

What went wrong, and what I would change

The board plays fine, but two cosmetic defects are visible up close.

DefectWhat happenedNext time
Notch in one cornerI turned a handwheel the wrong way during a milling pass, and the cutter took a small notch out of one corner of the board.Check the direction of travel with the cutter clear of the part before engaging, and set table stops on finishing passes.
Uneven, rounded chamfersI cut the chamfers by hand with a file, with the board held in a vise. I did not keep the file at a constant angle, so the edges came out rounded instead of flat and crisp.Cut the chamfers on the mill with a chamfer tool so every edge has the same angle and width.

Drawing first, then the shop

In the shop I worked from my own drawings, so each one had to carry everything needed to make the part: views, dimensions, hole and thread callouts, tolerances, and material.

  • Chair assembly. A six-part aluminum chair held with #4-40 stainless screws. I designed it, produced a six-sheet package with an exploded view, bill of materials, and part numbers, and then machined it.
  • Dovetail slide block. Orthographic views with a 45° dovetail, counterbored holes, and a tolerance block, in 4140 steel.
  • Cradle mount. Uses auxiliary and section views, because the angled faces cannot be dimensioned from the standard three.
  • Slider clamp. Slot, counterbore, and thread callouts on a clamping part.
Why it mattersA drawing is the contract between design and manufacturing. If a dimension is missing, the machinist has to guess, even when the machinist is me.
Self-directed CAD

Models built to learn a feature

Helical wind turbine. I modelled one blade by sweeping a profile along a helix, thickened it, and repeated it with a circular pattern. Building one blade and patterning it means a change to the blade updates all of them.

AC generator. A twelve-part model for a physics project showing the magnets, the rotating coil, and the drive shaft, built to explain how rotation in a magnetic field produces alternating current.
